TiltTwin

Remote CAD teaching through physical motion
Design process report

Kevin, Ayushi and Prince
CPSC 581, Fall 2026, Group Project 2: Expertise Sharing
Source: github.com/Kevin398/CPSC581-TiltTwin  |  Live system: tilttwin.vercel.app

Summary. An expert on a laptop demonstrates a rotation of a CAD part. A beginner on a phone reproduces it by physically turning the phone, so the virtual part turns as if it were held in their hand. Both people see live angle, direction and colour feedback, and both get a ✓ when the beginner matches.
Expertise conveyed: Spatial understanding of 3D rotation, meaning which axis, which direction and how far. Novices in Blender or Fusion struggle with this because the mouse mapping (orbit vs. rotate, gimbal axes) is abstract.
Configuration: One expert (laptop), one novice (smartphone), synchronous and remote, joined by a 4-letter room code.

We alternated divergent and convergent phases over five iterations. Each iteration produced an artifact (sketch set, storyboard, wireframe or prototype) that was reviewed before the next step. Kevin developed the initial sketches and baseline prototype, regularly consulting teammates Ayushi and Prince for feedback. 2. Divergence: ten interaction concepts

Each concept answers the same question in a different way: how can an expert's knowledge of 3D orientation reach a novice through the body rather than through words or a shared screen?

Collaborative sketch evaluation. Kevin produced the initial concept sketches and shared them with Ayushi and Prince for feedback. This provided a team perspective during early exploration rather than relying only on individual judgment. The concepts were then compared for embodied interaction, their ability to communicate CAD expertise, and their feasibility within the project timeline. The sketching process helped the team narrow the problem before committing to implementation.

3. Convergence: choosing the concept

Our first direction, CADSense, was a mouse-driven tutor covering extrude, rotate and scale. The beginner followed on-screen arrows to match a target model. When we checked it against the brief and rubric (25% of the grade is creative embodied input and output, and video calls or screen sharing as the core interaction are explicitly discouraged), CADSense's mouse input looked conventional. We scored the strongest concepts from Fig. 1 against criteria taken directly from the brief.

#5 Rotate a physical proxy object 3 2 3 1 1 10

#2 + #7 + #10 → TiltTwin (phone is the part) 3 3 3 2 2 13

Table 1. Scores from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong). These are qualitative design judgments used to compare concepts against our project constraints, not measured usability results. The proxy-object idea (#5) scored high on embodiment, but it requires tracking hardware or computer vision that would be difficult to implement reliably within our timeline. A smartphone already contains orientation sensors, allowing the phone itself to become the physical proxy.

Decision. Merge #2 (physical rotation), #7 (rotate until correct) and #10 (expert creates actions to copy) into TiltTwin. We deliberately gave up breadth (extrude and scale) to make one embodied interaction, rotation, complete and polished. This also enables a controlled comparison with mouse-based rotation in a future A/B study (§9).

4. Iteration 1: refining TiltTwin

Kept: 1, 3, 4, 5. Dropped: 2, the transparency overlay. On a phone-sized screen, two overlapping, semi-transparent L-brackets are hard to read. It becomes unclear which edges belong to which part, and when the part is close to the target the two shapes merge. The overlay also invites "matching shapes by eye" rather than understanding which axis and which direction, which is the expertise we want to convey. We replaced it with three cues that each answer one question:

  • Where should it end up? A small window showing the expert's demonstration: the part animating from 0° to the target on a loop.
  • Which way do I turn? A coloured arc arrow around the highlighted axis, from the current angle to the target.
  • How far am I? A large live gauge ("22° / 45°") plus colour coding: orange when far, yellow when close, green when matched.

Kevin shared the refinement sketches with Ayushi and Prince for feedback during this design stage. The final direction prioritized readable mobile visuals and explicit guidance about rotation direction and angle, rather than overlapping transparent models.

5. Iteration 2: storyboard and wireframes

v1 → v2 changes (Fig. 4a → 4b): the progress indicator changed from a vague "show progress" button to a live angle on the model itself, shown to both people. Showing "30°/45°" to both gives the expert a precise thing to correct ("15° more") and gives the novice an exact goal. v2 also makes physically rotating the phone the explicit input, replacing on-screen controls.

6. Iteration 3: prototypes

Prototype v1: mouse baseline (CADSense-style, "Version A")

What v1 taught us. Free 2-axis rotation made instructions hard to express ("rotate it like… this"). Feedback only came when the beginner pressed a button. Running in one tab meant there was no remote collaboration yet. These became the requirements for v2: one axis per lesson, continuous feedback and real two-device sync.

Collaborative prototyping. Kevin implemented the initial browser-based prototype and shared the evolving interaction with Ayushi and Prince for feedback. The prototype gave the team something concrete to evaluate beyond sketches and wireframes. This process helped identify the limitations of free-form mouse rotation, delayed angle checking, and the absence of communication between separate devices. The next iteration focused on clearer lessons, continuous feedback, and real-time synchronization.

7. Iteration 4: from "match an angle" to "make it fit"

After implementing the core phone-rotation interaction, we explored how to make TiltTwin more useful in real CAD work and more engaging, without weakening the embodied interaction. We compared several possible additions and selected features that strengthened the learning experience while remaining feasible within the project timeline:

CandidateValueCost or riskDecision
Part libraryBeginners practise on parts they will actually modelLowBuilt
Assembly "fit" task with a mating partGives the rotation a purpose: in CAD assemblies you orient a part so it mates with anotherMediumBuilt
Hot/cold vibrationFeel the answer without looking: strong embodied outputiPhone Safari has no vibration APIBuilt, with sound as the guaranteed channel
Stars and streaksFun and motivatingLow, but weak link to expertise sharingDeferred
  • Mating parts, not a ghost. Each part has a feature (a hole, an outlet or the jaws), and a solid mating part (a bolt, pipe, pin or nut) is fixed where that feature will be at the target. This keeps the decision from §4: no transparent overlay. It also turns an abstract angle into a physical goal, and the moment of success becomes "it clicks into place". A unit test checks that no feature sits on an X/Y/Z axis line; otherwise rotating about that axis would never visibly move it, and the task would be impossible to see.
  • Hot/cold guidance. While a lesson is active, the phone ticks. The ticks speed up (from ~1 to ~9 per second) and rise in pitch as the part gets closer, and stop when it fits. On Android each tick is also a vibration pulse. On iPhone we use the light haptic that iOS 18+ gives when a native switch control is toggled, because Safari has no vibration API. That trick is undocumented, so sound is the primary fallback where audio playback is permitted. The beginner can find the target by ear and by feel while their eyes are on the part. A sound button turns it off.
  • Expert side. The expert chooses the part as well as the axis and angle. The demonstration loop and live mirror both display the mating part. The session log records the selected part, allowing a future A/B usability study to compare interaction performance across different CAD components.

9. Evaluation

9.1 Technical verification (completed)

  • Automated sensor-mapping tests. The project includes tests for angle wrap-around at ±180°, quaternion twist extraction, and the mapping between physical phone movement and virtual CAD rotation. The tests cover portrait and both landscape phone orientations, checking the expected direction of rotation along X, Y and Z. Additional tests verify that the assembly features move visibly around each rotation axis. These tests helped validate the mathematical foundation of the interaction.
  • Simulated sensor testing. During development, simulated deviceorientation events were used to exercise the beginner's rotation controls and feedback states. The expert's synchronized view was also tested using separate browser tabs.
  • Bug identified and corrected. An earlier prototype had an issue where repeated synchronization updates restarted the completion timer, preventing the matched state from activating consistently. The timer was updated to use the lesson ID rather than the changing lesson object.
  • Real-iPhone testing — October 20, 2026. Prince ran the deployed app with the expert view on a second device and an iPhone as the beginner, using phone motion. The expert's session log recorded X +90° completed in 8.1 s and Z +45° in 4.2 s. On the Y lesson, however, Prince moved the phone the opposite way from what the hint intended (see the test-driven change in §9.2). After the fix, a re-test confirmed that X, Y and Z all moved in the expected direction. A second session that day tested the four parts and the hot/cold guidance on the iPhone: each part fitted its mating part when matched, and the hot/cold sound worked.
  • Real-device testing — October 21, 2026. Kevin tested the deployed application with an iPad acting as the expert and a smartphone acting as the beginner. Both devices used Safari. The devices successfully connected through a shared lesson session. The beginner used physical phone-motion controls to rotate the virtual CAD part, and the observed movements behaved in the expected directions. Communication between the expert and beginner devices worked during the test.
Verification outcome. The real-device walkthrough demonstrated that TiltTwin's core expertise-sharing interaction works beyond a simulated browser environment. An expert can deliver a CAD rotation task remotely, while the beginner manipulates a virtual model through physical phone movement. This establishes functional feasibility on the tested devices, but does not by itself establish learning effectiveness or compatibility with every smartphone and browser.

9.2 Pilot study and proposed A/B comparison

Completed exploratory evaluation. Our initial hands-on evaluation focused on whether TiltTwin's central interaction could be performed successfully using two separate devices. Two real-device sessions (expert device + iPhone on Oct 20; iPad + smartphone in Safari on Oct 21) confirmed session communication and the expected physical-to-virtual rotation behaviour, and the first session produced a design change (below). These were team walkthroughs, so we followed them with a small pilot study with three participants.

Pilot study: phone-tilt condition (n = 3). Three participants (P1–P3) each joined a separate lesson session on their phone (codes FR2A, 89ZM and H2UM), then completed three rotation tasks by physically turning the phone. A team member acted as the expert, sending each task from the expert view and watching the live mirror. Tasks covered all four parts, all three axes, and positive and negative angles of 45° and 90°. Completion time is recorded automatically by the expert's session log, from the moment the instruction arrives to the moment the part is first held on target (the 0.8 s hold is excluded).

Findings.

  • Every task was completed, on every axis, including the Y axis whose hint had been misread before it was reworded (see the box below). Mean times were similar across axes (X 3.9 s, Y 3.8 s, Z 3.8 s), so no axis stood out as harder.
  • Possible warm-up effect. The first task was the slowest for P1 and P2 (6.5 s and 5.1 s). Their later tasks averaged 3.1 s. Three participants is too few to separate learning from task difficulty.
  • Unexpected: the part keeps moving after "Fits!". In all three sessions, the expert's live readout had drifted far from the target after the success message: −119° on a −45° task, −23° on a +90° task, −37° on a −90° task (visible in Fig. 11a–c). The likely cause is that participants relaxed or lowered the phone once the task felt finished, and the mirror kept following the phone. For the expert this looks like an error next to a ✓. Change made after the pilot: once the part fits, it now stays snapped to the target pose on both screens (the beginner's view and the expert's mirror) until the next instruction arrives. We verified this by fitting a part and then turning the phone 150° away: both screens stayed at the target, and the next instruction released the part back to live tracking.

Proposed A/B comparison (next step). The pilot measured only the phone-tilt condition. To evaluate whether physical phone rotation helps beginners understand spatial manipulation, we designed a within-subjects comparison between two input methods:

  • Condition A — Touch/mouse dragging: The beginner rotates the CAD part using the application's drag controls.
  • Condition B — Physical phone rotation: The beginner completes equivalent tasks by physically rotating a smartphone.

Participants would complete comparable rotation tasks in both conditions, such as Y +45°, X −90° and Z +90°. The order would be counterbalanced to reduce learning effects. An expert would send the instructions and observe each participant's progress.

Test-driven change: the Y-axis hint. This is the clearest case of real-device testing changing the design.
StepWhat happened
ObservedOn the iPhone, X and Z lessons were completed by following the hints (8.1 s, 4.2 s), but on Y the tester moved the phone the opposite way to the one intended.
DiagnosedWe first suspected the sensor maths. We simulated the phone held in portrait and in both landscape directions, made each hint's motion, and checked the sign on every axis. The maths was correct in all three holds (now automated tests). The problem was the wording: "Turn the phone to the left" can mean the phone turning left (its screen ends up facing right) or the screen turning to face left. The X and Z hints ("tilt the top toward you", "counter-clockwise") can only be read one way, which explains why only Y failed.
ChangedThe hint now names a specific edge of the phone: "Swing the right edge of the phone away from you" (and "toward you" for negative angles).
Re-testedSame iPhone, same day: X, Y and Z all moved in the expected direction.

Lesson: for embodied instructions, describe the motion by a physical landmark on the device, not by a direction word that depends on the reader's frame of reference.

Evaluation limitations. The pilot is small (n = 3), has no drag condition to compare against, and did not record wrong-direction starts, ratings or preferences. It shows that beginners can complete TiltTwin's rotation tasks quickly, in about 4 s each, on every axis. It does not show that phone tilt is faster, easier or better for learning than mouse input; that is what the proposed A/B comparison would test.

10. Known limitations and incomplete functionality

  • One axis per lesson (by design). Compound rotations would be a sequence of lessons.
  • Rotation only: no extrusion or scaling, and no import of real Blender or Fusion files. There are four predefined parts (bracket, pipe elbow, gear, wrench).
  • iPhone haptics rely on an undocumented iOS 18+ behaviour and may not fire on older iOS. Hot/cold sound always works, but is muted when the iPhone's silent switch is on.
  • One expert and one beginner per room. A second beginner would overwrite the first one's progress.
  • Y-axis drift: turning left/right relies on the gyroscope and compass and can drift slowly on some phones. Press Calibrate to fix it.
  • Screen auto-rotation: large Z-axis turns can make the phone flip its layout unless rotation lock is on. The mapping stays correct, but the HUD jumps.
  • Security: rooms are open to anyone with the code, and the database rules only validate shape. This is acceptable for a class demo, not for production.
  • The expert's session log lives in that browser tab and is lost on reload; use "Copy CSV" to keep it.
